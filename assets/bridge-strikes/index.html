Which low bridges should the city fix first?

Trucks hit low bridges on New York City roads 943 times between 2013 and 2025. 92% of those hits were on roads that are not marked as truck routes. I wanted to find where a small budget would help the most.

Do past strikes point to the next ones?

For each year from 2016 to 2025, I pretended I only knew the years before it. I picked the bridges with the most strikes so far, then counted how many of that year's strikes happened at them. Use the slider, or the arrow keys, to change how many bridges get fixed. You can also tick which lines to show.

Three bridges changed by more than luck would explain. Hutchinson River Parkway at Westchester Avenue went from about 14 strikes a year to 3, starting in 2019. Belt Parkway at 17th Avenue had about 6 a year, then none from 2023. Its last strike was Nov 15, 2022. Henry Hudson Parkway at West 239th Street had none through 2016, then about 4 a year. I do not know why any of this happened. A bridge that jumps up one year often calms down on its own, so a drop does not prove a fix worked. A jump from zero might just mean better reporting.

Most strikes are off the truck routes

72% of strikes were on parkways, and parkways are almost never marked as truck routes. The data cannot say why trucks are there, so I would first look at how trucks end up on these roads, at signs and at enforcement, before taller bridges. Only 78 strikes were on marked truck routes. About half were on four roads: Brooklyn-Queens Expressway, Cross Bronx Expressway, Queens Boulevard and Long Island Expressway. For those, the bridge or the road itself may need a closer look.

85% of strikes happen Monday to Friday, which fits trucks that are working. I found no clear season.

Strikes come from one direction

At most of the busiest bridges, nearly all strikes happen in one direction of travel. A sign or a sensor could go on that side, before the bridge. The data does not say exactly what the direction column means.

What the data cannot tell you

  • The file has no bridge height, truck type, damage, cost or traffic count. So I cannot give rates or savings.
  • Some reports may be missing.
  • Every report is dated at midnight, so I could look at the day of the week and the month, but not the time of day.
  • The driver's license state is blank in 37% of reports, and how often it was filled in changed over the years. I only looked at 2019 to 2024, when New York licenses were 16% of the 354 reports that had one. I did not draw any conclusion from that.
  • 35 of 943 reports have no bridge ID, so they are not in the bridge counts. Street names in the file are not consistent, so I used the most common pair for each bridge. A few labels, like FDR Drive near East 61st Street, cover a stretch of road, not one spot.
  • I did not use machine learning. With 943 reports and only a location to go on, a model would not be trustworthy.
