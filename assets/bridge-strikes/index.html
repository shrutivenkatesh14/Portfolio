Which low bridges should the city fix first?

Trucks hit low bridges on New York City roads 943 times between 2013 and 2025. Most of those roads are not truck routes. I used the city's strike reports to decide where a limited budget does the most good.

How much does the ranking cover?

Each year from 2016 to 2025 I ranked sites using only earlier years, then counted how many of that year's strikes fell at the top ones. Drag the slider to change how many sites are funded. Click a name in the legend to hide or show a line.

Ranking on all history is about as good as ranking on the last three years or the last year. In the ten test years the all-history ranking came out ahead of the last-three-years ranking in 6, by small margins, so I read it as a tie. The gap to the best possible ranking is the cost of not knowing next year.

Hotspots move

Strikes per year at the ten sites with the most strikes. Total strikes in brackets. A site is one bridge, or several bridge IDs within 30 m on the same road.

Three sites changed in a way chance does not explain (a test that allows for picking the best year, with false discoveries controlled). Hutchinson River Parkway at Westchester Avenue fell from about 14 strikes a year to 3 from 2019. Belt Parkway at 17th Avenue had about 6 a year, then none from 2023; its last strike was Nov 15, 2022. Henry Hudson Parkway at West 239th Street went from none through 2016 to about 4 a year. I do not know why. Sites picked for a spike often calm down by chance, so a drop is not proof that anything worked, and a jump from zero may be better reporting.

Two different problems

Parkways are almost never marked as truck routes, so most strikes there are trucks on roads they should not be on. That is a routing, signage or enforcement problem. The 78 strikes on truck routes sit mostly on a few other roads and are a clearance or geometry problem.

85% of strikes happen Monday to Friday, which fits commercial traffic. There is no strong seasonal pattern.

Strikes come from one direction

At most of the busiest sites nearly every strike is in one direction of travel, so detection or signage can go on one approach, upstream of the bridge. The file does not say exactly what the direction field records.

What the data cannot say

  • The file has no clearance height, vehicle type, damage, cost or traffic volume. So there are no rates and no savings claims.
  • Reports are not guaranteed complete.
  • Time of day is always midnight, so only day of week and month can be studied.
  • The driver-license state is missing in 37% of rows and its reporting changed over time. I only looked at 2019 to 2024, where New York licenses were 16% of 354 known rows, and drew no conclusion from it.
  • 35 of 943 rows have no bridge ID and are left out of the site counts. Street labels in the file are inconsistent, so each site uses its most common street pair. Labels for a few sites, such as FDR Drive near East 61st Street, cover a stretch of road.
  • No machine learning: 943 rows and no features beyond location cannot support a credible model.
